# Introducing an LLM screening assistant into our review team: an experience report

## Abstract

This is an experience report describing how our review team added a language-model screening assistant to an ongoing scoping review. We describe the procedure we followed, the checks we put in place, and what we observed over four months. We did not run a controlled comparison, and the numbers below should be read as description rather than evidence.

## Methods

Our scoping review concerned digital mental-health interventions. After the database search returned 6,300 records and deduplication left 4,900, we configured a screening assistant built on a commercial language model. The assistant read each title and abstract against our inclusion criteria and proposed a label.

One team member reviewed every proposed label for the first 500 records to build trust, then moved to reviewing only the assistant's "include" and "borderline" proposals plus a 10 percent sample of its "exclude" proposals. There was no second independent human screener and no comparator condition; this was a single-site change to our normal workflow.

We logged every case where a human overturned the assistant's label and discussed a sample of them at weekly meetings.

## Results

Over four months the assistant labelled 4,900 records. Humans overturned 6 percent of the labels they reviewed. Most overturned cases were the assistant marking a qualitative study "exclude" because our criteria were written with quantitative designs in mind — a problem with our criteria, not the assistant. We did not estimate recall against a reference standard, because we had none.

The team reported that screening felt faster and less draining, but we did not measure time formally.

## Discussion

We cannot say from this report whether the assistant improved or harmed the review. What we can say is that introducing it surfaced ambiguities in our own inclusion criteria that had gone unnoticed for years. A reader could reasonably judge this report as useful evidence about implementation, or as too uncontrolled to count as evidence about screening performance at all.
