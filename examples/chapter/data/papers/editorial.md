# We are automating the wrong parts of the review

## Editorial

I have spent twenty years conducting systematic reviews, and I want to sound a note of caution about the current enthusiasm for automating them.

Every month brings another tool that promises to screen abstracts, extract data, or draft a synthesis. The demonstrations are impressive. But a demonstration is not an evaluation, and the field is accumulating tools far faster than it is accumulating evidence that they are safe to use.

My worry is not that the models make mistakes. Humans make mistakes too. My worry is that the mistakes are different in kind. A tired human reviewer misses a study because they skimmed the abstract. A language model misses a study because the abstract phrased the outcome in a way that fell outside the pattern it had learned, and it will miss every study phrased that way, silently and consistently.

I am also troubled by how the labour is shifting. The parts of a review that are genuinely hard — framing the question, judging whether two studies are similar enough to combine, deciding what the evidence means — are exactly the parts that are not being automated. What is being automated is the tedious middle. That is welcome, but we should be honest that it does not shorten the intellectually demanding work; it just removes the breaks.

This piece offers no data and no method. It is one reviewer's opinion, written to provoke a discussion at the point where that discussion is still possible.
